My [last post](/blog/posts/new-kind-of-computer/) ended with a dare. Spin up an agent sandbox, suspend it mid-session, restore it, and count how many of your assumptions come back broken. This one is for the team on the other side of the dare, the one running the fleet. Maybe that's a Substrate deployment on your own clusters, maybe it's an estate of Lambda MicroVMs in your own AWS account, maybe a BYOC vendor put either shape inside your VPC last quarter. You didn't write the platform and you can't read the workload, but the serving layer between them is software you operate, and a spec already exists for operating software safely enough to hand off. [Accessible Ops](https://accessibleops.net/) is fourteen properties that make infrastructure safe to give to a competent stranger, human or agent. I co-authored it, so read this post as me checking whether our own spec survives contact with the new computer.

One line makes the spec usable here. The contents of a sandbox sit outside your boundary. The agent's working tree, its shell history, whatever the model installed at 2am, no property of any spec will make that readable, and the last post already argued why. Everything up to the isolation boundary is yours. The scheduler, the checkpoint store, the session database, the reaper, the credential minter. Inside that line the fourteen properties apply at full strength, and they bend in interesting places, because ten years of container discipline assumed cattle and this is the first platform whose operators keep thousands of pets they aren't allowed to read.

## The reaper runs tonight

Somewhere in your platform is a job that reclaims idle sandboxes, because parked sessions are what make the economics work and storage eventually wants its blocks back. On any other fleet that job is hygiene. On this one it's a mass-deletion engine pointed at your users' half-finished work, because idle is the normal state of user data here. A session that hasn't woken in three weeks might be abandoned, or it might be the conversation a developer reopens tomorrow expecting the terminal scrollback and the working tree to be right where everything was left.

[Manage only what you declare](https://accessibleops.net/manage-only-what-you-declare/) is the property that keeps the reaper survivable. A delete gates on an ownership marker the resource itself carries, never on an inference from silence. The spec wrote that rule so a newcomer's half-written config can't become a purge, and your reaper is the same purge with a cron schedule. It deserves the same gate, an explicit expiry the user agreed to, stamped on the sandbox itself, plus a soft-delete window long enough to survive the reaper's worst day.

The session database is the other half of the same risk. Your platform keeps a store mapping session to host to checkpoint, and [the live system is the truth](https://accessibleops.net/truth-lives-live/) warns about that store by name, an authoritative second copy you now have to stand up, secure, and repair. The day it disagrees with what's actually restorable, someone's three-week-old session doesn't come back, and the reconciliation runbook was never written because nobody believed the two could diverge. Treat the store as an index you can rebuild by walking the platform and divergence is an inconvenience. Treat it as the truth and divergence is data loss.

## Resume is a deploy

A checkpoint is an artifact. It gets built on one host, ages in storage for days or weeks, and ships into production the moment the user's next message lands. The market talks about restore purely as a speed number, Sprites checkpoint a whole filesystem in about 300 milliseconds and Daytona spins up in under 90, and nobody publishes what restore verifies. For most platforms the honest answer is a checksum.

[Verify the artifact](https://accessibleops.net/verify-the-artifact/) reads like it was written about a container registry, and it maps onto the checkpoint word for word. The deployable carries its origin and contents, its provenance, and a signature tied to a named identity, and the check that admits it runs before apply and fails closed. Swap apply for restore and you have the spec for a resume path. Which host wrote this checkpoint, under which platform version, for which session, acting for which user, and does the signature still chain to an identity you trust. If your restore path can't answer those questions, then anything that can write to your checkpoint bucket can put a process into production with no deploy, no diff, and no review, wearing the name of a session your user trusts.

The stakes run higher than they do in CI, too. A container image at least descends from a Dockerfile somebody merged. A checkpoint's contents were authored at runtime by a model, so the envelope's provenance is the only provenance that will ever exist. The last post argued that building the sandbox image yourself buys you nothing because the interesting code arrives at runtime. The operator's corollary is that everything you can verify lives in the wrapper, so the wrapper has to carry all of it.

[Reversible before risky](https://accessibleops.net/reversible-before-risky/) is the other property the resume path pulls in. The checkpoint format is a contract between versions of your platform, and a fleet upgrade under ten thousand suspended sessions changes the reader of every one of them. If the new scheduler writes snapshots the old one can't read, your rollback ended the moment the first post-upgrade checkpoint hit storage, and you find out during the incident that made you want to roll back. The spec sorts changes into the ones that run on their own and the ones that wait for a human, by whether they can be undone. Checkpoint-format migrations sit in the second pile with room to spare. The routine-looking platform upgrade sits there too, until someone checks which pile it actually belongs in.

## The 3am page

Now the pager side. AWS caps the total memory of your MicroVMs per region, suspended and running alike, so the parked sessions that make your economics work draw down the same quota as the live ones. One team parks a few hundred sessions on a Friday experiment, and Monday morning nobody in the region can resume. No deploy happened and there's no diff to revert, because the dangerous unit on this fleet is an accumulation of sleeping state. [Bounded blast radius](https://accessibleops.net/bounded-blast-radius/) still applies, aimed at tenants instead of changes. A per-team ceiling under the regional quota means the newcomer's worst day exhausts their own budget and leaves everyone else's morning alone.

Capacity lore is the quieter half of the same page. Somewhere in your fleet is the ratio that makes the numbers work. Substrate's demo packs about 250 sessions onto 8 pods, and whoever tuned your version of that number has reasons involving wake latency, memory pressure, and the shape of your users' working day. [Documentation is law](https://accessibleops.net/documentation-is-law/) exists for that number. A schema tells a newcomer what a valid session spec looks like, and only prose can say why the ratio is 31, what breaks at 40, and which alert fires first on the way there. A newcomer, human or agent, will deduce the wrong ratio with total confidence, because 31 looks like an accident until someone writes down that it isn't.

## No console surgery on pets

The container era's one path to prod got an assist from fungibility. Nobody hand-fixes a replica when the fix is to delete it and let the deployment stamp a fresh copy. That escape hatch is gone. When agent-session-1 wedges and a user is waiting on that machine specifically, deleting it destroys the thing they came back for, so the temptation is surgery, a shell on the host, a hand-edit to the session record, a live poke at the one pet that matters. [One path to prod](https://accessibleops.net/one-path-to-prod/) is hardest to keep exactly where instances stop being interchangeable, and that's also where it pays the most, because the fleet is too big for anyone to remember which pets got hand-modified. Emergency access can exist, and it works like a break-glass path, logged, diffed after the fact, and rare enough to review individually. [Attributable](https://accessibleops.net/attributable/) does the accounting, every sandbox traces to the user it acts for, every intervention traces to the operator who made it, and each pet carries credentials minted at session start and revocable one at a time, so cutting one loose never means rotating everything.

[Honor the lower layer](https://accessibleops.net/honor-the-lower-layer/) has an operator's reading here too. The property asks what record a competent stranger can read to predict what the platform will do. On a KubeMicroVM fleet the answer is the CRD, so keep the rendered resources where that stranger can find them. Substrate takes kube-scheduler out of the session path on purpose, seconds matter when a human is waiting, which means the manifest stopped being the record of anything that matters. The question to put to your deployment is which readable record replaced it. If the answer is a gRPC endpoint and a dashboard, property one is pointing at where your postmortems will start.

## The spec came first this time

Kubernetes shipped a working primitive in 2015 and spent the next five years buying its operational discipline one postmortem at a time. I said last time that the sandbox sits at the same spot on that curve. The difference worth using is that a written spec exists before the incident reports do. So here's the operator's version of the dare. Trace your reaper's delete path and name the marker that gates it. Trace your restore path and name what it verifies beyond a checksum. Where either answer comes up empty, you've found one of this decade's postmortems early, while it still costs a design review.
