How I run a team

Each with the receipt behind it.

1

Agree on the standard before the work starts

How the work gets done, what good looks like, how we treat each other. A new hire learns the norms before the tasks, and a new team aligns on them before anything ships. When someone falls short, the conversation starts from the goal the team agreed to. Training, remediation, and firing decisions get something solid to stand on.

Receipt

At SocialDrizzle I ran engineering and product with a team of peers fresh out of college. Once we had agreed that perfect is the enemy of good, saying it again kept everyone moving. Without that agreement, a team hearing it over and over would only hear a manager asking for less than their best.

2

Learn each person

The work runs through people with lives, and ignoring that does not make it work out. Trouble with someone means finding out what the trouble is. I used to fire fast, on the theory that a team trusts teammates who always deliver. Now I know some things take longer than a month to clear, and the team will tell me when someone is actually a problem.

Receipt

At the steakhouse the cook who came in late was frazzled, and a minute to breathe before the rush was cheaper than a cut hand or a burned plate. When someone did have to go, the rest lost a friend and worried about their own jobs. A standard everyone had agreed to up front meant they knew why it happened and where they stood.

3

Sit down, shut up, and leave silence

The best idea in the room can come from anyone, and it is often sitting in someone’s throat waiting for a gap. I build silence into meetings. Wait ten seconds after asking for questions, give someone a moment to unmute, and don’t jump in when a person pauses to breathe.

Receipt

Raunak Singwi once offered me a moment when I lost my train of thought on a call, and then actually stayed quiet. The idea came back the second the time pressure left. I have run meetings that way since.

4

Make the change something people want

I do not tell anyone to do something I would not do myself. When I am asking a team to change how it works, the new way has to be better for the people doing the work, and they have to see it coming.

Receipt

At CyberGRX the new deploy process was pleasant to use, PRs spent less time in queue, and people got back to the interesting work. I announced it ahead of time, ran tiered onboardings and demos, and had people block the time on their calendars. Fifty engineers were shipping with it inside 3 days.
