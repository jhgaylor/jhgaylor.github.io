How I run a team

Each with the receipt behind it.

1

Agree on the standard before the work starts

When a team forms, we agree on how the work gets done and what good looks like. New hires hear those expectations out loud, even though they will see them modeled by their teammates. When someone falls short, they know how it lands on the team, and any conversation about training, remediation, or letting go has something to stand on.

Receipt

At SocialDrizzle I ran engineering and product with peers fresh out of college. Once we had agreed that perfect is the enemy of good, saying it again kept everyone moving. Without the agreement, it would have sounded like a manager asking for less than their best.

2

Learn each person

The work runs through people with lives. By knowing each one, I know which problem will excite someone, and I hear early when something is off. I used to fire fast, on the theory that a team trusts teammates who always deliver. Now I wait for the team to tell me someone is really a problem before I act, because some things take longer than a month to clear.

Receipt

A cook came in late one night at the steakhouse, and I knew they would be wound up about it. I took them aside, said the work could wait, and we took a minute before service. That was cheaper than a cut hand or a burned plate.

3

Sit down, shut up, and leave silence

The best idea in the room can come from anyone, and it is often unsaid, waiting for a gap to interject or for a little more prodding. So I wait ten seconds after asking for questions, give people time to unmute, and don’t jump in when someone pauses to breathe.

Receipt

When I lost my train of thought on a call, Raunak Singwi insisted I take a moment, and he meant it. The idea came back the second the pressure left. I have run meetings that way since.

4

Make the change something people want

I have watched teams reorged every quarter with no warning, drop everything and report to someone new. Each round eroded trust and morale that nobody was counting. I have watched leaders force a system that was wrong for the team, and the team resist it, because smart people given an objective, the resources, and room to operate will get there themselves. So any change I make has to be better for the people doing the work, and they see it coming.

Receipt

At CyberGRX the new deploy process was pleasant to use, PRs spent less time in queue, and people got back to the interesting work. I announced it ahead of time, ran demos, and had people block the time. Fifty engineers were shipping with it inside 3 days.
